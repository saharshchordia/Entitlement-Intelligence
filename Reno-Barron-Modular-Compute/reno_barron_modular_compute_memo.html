Bottom line: the parcel is not the problem. The politics and current application gate are the problem. Reno's official development-services page says the City Council approved a data-center moratorium on June 1, 2026; the moratorium runs through August 31, 2027 or until adequate regulations are adopted, and the city will not accept conditional use permit requests for data centers during that window.

The easiest path is therefore not an immediate public CUP filing. It is a two-track strategy: first, obtain a written staff classification for the exact modular-compute operating facts; second, if staff treats the use as a data center, spend the moratorium period building a post-moratorium CUP record that is materially smaller, cleaner, quieter, and more grid-responsible than the projects that triggered the 2025-2026 controversy.

Use Classification

For entitlement purposes, describe the project by what it does, not by the packaging label. If modular units house servers, high-density computing equipment, cooling equipment, network infrastructure, battery systems, transformers, generators, or continuous remote-operation loads, Reno staff may reasonably route the use to the data-center line item even if the project is smaller than a hyperscale facility.

Local Rules That Matter

Reno's code table lists "Data Center" under Storage, Distribution, and Warehousing and points to specific use standards. The data-center standards limit dock doors to three per building and require loading docks and semi-truck staging to minimize noise and odor impacts on residential properties; certain screening alternatives and CUP modifications are identified in the standard.

The conditional-use-permit findings matter more than the base zoning label. A CUP record will need to prove compatibility, adequate public services and facilities, and no material detriment to health, safety, and welfare. For this project, the practical proof set is power-service responsibility, no water-cooling burden, low nighttime noise, limited generator testing, limited truck activity, screening, fire/electrical safety, and a neighborhood-facing operating plan.

Moratorium And Application Gate

Reno's official position as of the September 15, 2026 research date is direct: after the June 1, 2026 final moratorium resolution, the City will not accept data-center CUP requests through August 31, 2027, or until adequate regulations are adopted sooner. Power And Utility Path

Power is the front-door political issue in Reno. NV Energy's own planning materials discuss load growth from data centers and other large projects; TMRPA's regional materials highlight energy infrastructure and regional conformance thresholds for generation facilities of 5 MW or greater and transmission infrastructure of 60 kV or greater. The local debate is not just whether the site can be served; it is who pays and what infrastructure commitments the project triggers.

Recommended packet posture: prepare the first pre-application/classification package around a 1-2 MW phase-one cap, with a separate sensitivity ask up to 3 MW. Do not lead with a 5 MW+ request. Reno's February 26, 2025 Council data-center presentation made MW loads politically legible by comparing Keystone at 7 MW, Oppidan at 8 MW, Webb at 28.5 MW, Google in Washoe County at 360 MW, Storey County projects at 65-1,200 MW, and 600 homes at roughly 1 MW. A 1-2 MW first phase lets the sponsor position Barron as a small, capped pilot rather than another regional-load project.

Noise And Water

The easiest community-support path is a "quiet, dry, small, enclosed" operating story. TMRPA's research notes that continuous operation, lighting, security, and equipment noise make data centers more compatible with industrial/commercial areas than residential areas. This site has an industrial-commercial zoning baseline and an airport-noise overlay, but that does not eliminate local nuisance review.

Local Planning Evidence

The investor read from Reno's local record is that the city moved through three stages in roughly eighteen months: first, it defined data centers administratively; second, it tried to manage them through CUP conditions; third, it concluded that individual CUP conditions were not enough and paused new data-center applications while broader standards are drafted. That sequence matters more than any single comparable approval.

Local recordWhat Reno focused onInvestor strategy for 3705 Barron Way
Administrative definition and January 2025 CUP staff reports Reno's staff reports define data centers by digital-data storage, management, processing, transmission, servers, network equipment, cooling, generators, substations, and supporting infrastructure. Staff treated data centers as lower-truck-trip industrial uses, but still required CUP findings for compatibility, public services, and health/safety/welfare. Do not rely on the phrase "modular compute" to avoid the data-center frame. The first investor diligence step is a written classification package that states exactly what is inside the modules, the MW envelope, cooling method, generator/battery configuration, utility interconnection, water demand, staff count, and truck/service profile.
Oppidan 5MW Data Center staff packet, Jan. 15, 2025 Staff recommended approval for an IC-zoned data-center CUP because the site was in an industrial pattern, traffic was low, NV Energy had provided a power will-serve, TMWA/sewer service would be handled through permit-stage will-serve processes, truck activity was limited to 6 a.m.-11 p.m., generators were limited to outages/daytime testing, and acoustic/screening conditions were imposed near a mobile-home park. Barron should borrow the staff-proof structure, not the public posture. A future application should arrive with a will-serve/service-capacity narrative, generator-testing limits, no-idling/no-staging conditions, acoustic modeling, screen-wall/landscape exhibits, and a phase cap. The strongest position is not "Reno approved something similar"; it is "Reno's own prior condition set is the starting floor, and this project is lower impact."
Keystone Data Center staff packet and public comments, Jan. 15, 2025 Keystone showed that even small urban infill data-center projects can become symbolic fights. The staff report supported approval with extensive acoustic barriers, chiller attenuation, generator-use limits, lighting controls, EV/PV/low-flow conditions, and an NV Energy will-serve. Public comments still focused on power-grid strain, water, noise, generators, heat, property values, low jobs, tax economics, and distrust of the applicant/use description. The investor lesson is that technical compliance will not carry the hearing by itself. A Barron strategy should lead with "quiet, dry, capped, customer-funded, no crypto, no routine diesel, no water cooling" before opponents define the project as another data-center land grab. The package should be written for Council/community readers, not only for plan reviewers.
City Council data-center discussion packet, Feb. 26, 2025 The Council presentation compared Reno projects against larger regional data-center loads: Webb at 28.5 MW, Oppidan at 8 MW, Keystone at 7 MW, Google in Washoe County at 360 MW, and Storey County projects from 65 MW to 1,200 MW. It also put water, power, noise, building design, screening, lighting, distance from residents, and whether review should be amended directly before Council. Size the first phase to avoid looking like the next regional-load problem. The recommended packet ask is 1-2 MW, with 3 MW studied quietly as a utility sensitivity. Investors should underwrite a phase-one approval case and treat larger MW upside as a later political/regulatory option, not as base-case entitlement value.
Regional/TMRPA policy work and June 2026 moratorium TMRPA materials frame data centers as regional energy-load and land-use questions, not just parcel-use questions. Reno's June 1, 2026 moratorium converted that policy concern into an application gate: no new data-center CUP intake until August 31, 2027 or earlier adoption of adequate regulations. The current path is pre-development, not filing. Use the moratorium period to shape a regulation-ready package, meet staff and regional stakeholders, build the utility story, and decide whether the site is worth carrying through an uncertain rulemaking window.

The practical investment thesis is therefore conditional. The parcel's IC zoning, ME master-plan designation, existing industrial/flex context, and Ward 6 location make it more plausible than a mixed-use/residential-edge infill fight like Keystone. But Reno's meeting record shows that data-center politics are now about system impacts: who bears power-upgrade risk, whether scarce water is consumed for cooling, whether continuous equipment noise reaches nearby users, and whether the community gets enough fiscal/jobs benefit for the land and infrastructure burden.

For Barron, the lowest-friction posture is a deliberately small first phase with no evaporative/process-water cooling, no routine generator operation, distribution-level service if feasible, volunteered acoustic and screening conditions, and a public-facing expansion trigger. A sponsor should be prepared to say: "this is not a hyperscale data center, not crypto mining, not a water-cooled facility, not a request for ratepayer-funded infrastructure, and not a blank check for future MW growth."
