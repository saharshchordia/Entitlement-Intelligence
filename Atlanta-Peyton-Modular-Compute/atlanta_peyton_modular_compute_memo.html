Zoning feasibility is directionally favorable for a modular compute project at 2770 Peyton Rd NW, but classification discipline matters. The site maps as I-1 Light Industrial with Industrial future land use, no mapped zoning overlay, no mapped building moratorium, no mapped historic constraint, and no point-intersecting rezoning or special-use permit case. That is a materially better baseline than mixed-use, BeltLine, transit-adjacent, or residential-edge sites.

The main risk is not the base zoning district. The risk is Atlanta's political and code treatment of "data centers." In the City Utilities record, Chair Dustin Hillis stated that no data center would be built in Atlanta without Council approval and referenced a special-use-permit requirement even for properly zoned sites. A modular compute project should therefore be packaged around low water use, low noise, no crypto mining, no public-facing campus, limited traffic, screened equipment, and a phased electrical load profile.

  • Zoning posture: I-1 Light Industrial is a plausible district for enclosed compute, equipment, warehousing, light industrial, utility, and business-service style uses.
  • Most important gating question: whether Atlanta classifies the project as a "data center" despite modular form factor, no water cooling, and lower power demand.
  • Least-pushback / most-power band: target an initial utility request at or below 2 MW and a near-term total cap around 5-10 MW if distribution capacity is available without a visible substation or transmission upgrade.
  • Do not lead with: 25+ MW demand, 100 MW entitlement language, evaporative cooling, crypto/blockchain mining, round-the-clock outdoor equipment noise, or a use description that reads like a conventional hyperscale data center.

Modular Compute Classification

Atlanta's adopted data-center definition is functional: a facility engaged in storage, management, processing, or transmission of digital data that houses computer or network equipment, servers, appliances, or associated components. That definition may capture modular compute even if the project is smaller, phased, lower-power, and built without water cooling.

The strongest argument is not "this is not compute." The stronger argument is "this is an I-1 compatible modular compute facility with low external impacts, no water cooling, no crypto mining, limited traffic, and a modest phased load." The key diligence item is a written OZD use determination confirming whether the SUP requirement applies.

Local Rules That Matter

Current zoning is favorable because I-1 Light Industrial and Industrial future land use match the general character of an enclosed equipment and light-industrial operation. That does not eliminate data-center-specific controls, overlay checks, building/fire review, stormwater, utility coordination, noise, lighting, screening, or any Council/SUP path triggered by use classification.

Rule / Topic What It Means Application To Peyton
I-1 Light Industrial I-1 allows industrial, warehousing, business-service, office/lab, utility, and similar uses, but industrial operations must not create adverse effects beyond property boundaries, including excessive noise or vibration. Likely compatible if operations are enclosed and impact-controlled. A zoning verification request should frame the use in I-1 terms rather than as a hyperscale data center.

Ordinary bus stops are not necessarily high-capacity transit for this purpose. The site has nearby MARTA bus stops, but no mapped rail/streetcar/BRT high-capacity stop was identified at the point. Confirm with city staff in a zoning verification.

If OZD classifies modular compute as a data center, assume discretionary review even on I-1.

Moratorium And Application Gate

No point-intersecting building moratorium, active rezoning, or active SUP case appeared in the official Atlanta GIS layers for 2770 Peyton Rd NW. The practical application gate is different: if Atlanta classifies the use as a data center, the City Utilities record indicates Council/SUP review should be expected even where the base zoning is otherwise compatible.

The cleanest diligence sequence is to ask OZD for a written use determination before public filing, while the project is still described as modular compute with no water cooling, a low initial electrical request, enclosed operations, and no crypto mining.

Power And Utility Path

There is no Atlanta MW/kW zoning safe harbor found in the reviewed record. Power thresholds matter because larger loads make the project look more like a conventional data center, require more utility engineering, and increase the chance of political questions about grid capacity, ratepayer cost shifting, backup generation, and offsite infrastructure.

Requested Load Likely Utility / Political Texture Recommended Framing
<250 kW Lowest friction, but probably too small for the intended modular compute plan. Useful for temporary testing or office/light-industrial support load.
250-500 kW Still relatively quiet. Georgia Power rate materials reference 250 kW as a real-time-pricing eligibility threshold for certain customers. Good pilot band if the goal is to prove the use before asking for larger service.
500 kW-2 MW Begins to read as a serious commercial load. Georgia Power large-business tariffs generally start above 500 kW. Best first-phase band. Ask whether existing distribution can serve without major offsite upgrades.
2-10 MW Commercially meaningful but still materially below the 100 MW level used in Georgia Power large-load policy and recent Atlanta data-center negotiations. Best total near-term cap. Position as phased modular compute with no water cooling and a hard noise plan.
10-25 MW Harder. Likely to require more utility engineering, potential feeder or substation work, and more attention from city stakeholders. Use only if Georgia Power confirms a short, mostly existing-infrastructure path.
25-99 MW High scrutiny. It will likely be perceived as a data-center-scale project even if branded as modular compute. Avoid for a low-pushback zoning strategy.
100 MW+ Georgia Power applies explicit large-load customer protections at 100 MW and above, including upfront infrastructure cost, long-term contracts, collateral, minimum bills, and termination payments. Recent Atlanta draft zoning for a different site also used 100 MW as a facility cap. Highest pushback band. This is the wrong posture for a "sneak through moratoriums" strategy.

Recommended ask: start with a 1-2 MW phase and a 5-10 MW planning envelope, expressly conditioned on no water cooling, no crypto mining, no new transmission line, no visible substation if avoidable, and no property-line noise exceedance. That is the strongest "most power for least pushback" posture found from the available zoning and utility record.

Noise And Water

Noise: I-1 use language itself cares about impacts beyond property boundaries, including excessive noise and vibration. Recent Atlanta data-center negotiations outside this site also referenced compliance with the Atlanta Noise Ordinance. For modular compute, commission a pre-application acoustic memo covering fans, transformers, backup power, HVAC, tonal noise, nighttime operations, and nearest residential receptors.

Water: The user's stated no-water-cooling fact is highly valuable. On August 17 and August 25, 2026, Council and City Utilities read a data-center ordinance that would require closed-loop or substantially non-consumptive cooling and prohibit routine potable-water cooling for data centers served by Atlanta municipal water. Even if not yet final for this site, "no water cooling" should be front-page positioning.

Local Planning Evidence

Planning Record Signal Use In Strategy
Data-center zoning restrictions City Council approved ordinances defining data centers and restricting them near high-capacity transit and in the BeltLine Overlay. Shows Atlanta is comfortable regulating data centers through zoning, especially where land-use opportunity cost is high.
West End / Adair Park debate Public reporting and neighborhood sources show resistance to data centers near MARTA and mixed-use redevelopment areas. Mayor Dickens criticized data centers as potentially poor urban land use in those contexts. Peyton's industrial context is better, but the project should avoid sounding like a land-banking hyperscale campus.
Data Center Task Force referral Council referred the Data Center Task Force resolution back to committee by a 13-1 vote. In discussion, a councilmember said Atlanta should not be an anti-data-center city, but should get data centers right. There is a lane for a responsible compute project, but it needs to look deliberately controlled: smaller phased load, no water cooling, noise proof, and written commitments.
Water-cooling ordinance referral Council referred the water-cooling ordinance to City Utilities without objection. The item targets data centers in Atlanta or served by Atlanta water and would restrict routine potable-water cooling. No-water-cooling is not a side benefit; it is a core zoning-feasibility argument.
Neighborhood planning concerns A Sylvan Hills resident urged the task force to examine where data centers belong, infrastructure support, community impacts, capital markets/public finance, and enforceable commitments on jobs, water efficiency, energy use, and community benefits. For Peyton, bring enforceable facts instead of promises: max load/phasing, no water cooling, acoustic limits, screening, emergency operations, and utility-capacity letters.
Council/SUP posture Chair Dustin Hillis said no data center would be built in Atlanta without Council approval, because data centers must seek special-use permits even if properly zoned. He also said no data-center SUP applications had been filed since that legislation passed. This is the key feasibility issue. Ask OZD in writing whether the proposed modular compute use is a data center and whether the SUP requirement applies.
Pending policy work The committee held both the water-cooling ordinance and Data Center Task Force resolution. The task-force item was held while the sponsor coordinates with the administration on parallel task-force efforts. Rules are still moving. Filing as "generic data center" is high friction; filing as low-impact modular compute with a clear code interpretation is the cleaner path.
