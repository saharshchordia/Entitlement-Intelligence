4401 Deen Road is in Fort Worth, in Council District 2, and is zoned K Heavy Industrial. Under the current Fort Worth zoning table, "Data center" is a permitted use in industrial districts I, J, and K. That is the site's biggest advantage.

The risk is not classic zoning use permission. The risk is timing, classification, and political optics. Fort Worth is actively rewriting its data-center rules, has initiated a moratorium process, and now requires data-center applications to show grid-approval documentation before key city development applications can be processed. The City says the moratorium would not apply to vested, approved, or already-submitted zoning applications, but 4401 Deen has no public data-center filing found yet.

For modular compute, the best posture is not to argue that the site is "not a data center" by label. The better posture is factual: lower maximum load, no evaporative or water cooling, limited/no generators, low employee count, no crypto mining, small footprint, and compliance with emerging noise/screening standards even if not yet mandatory. If the equipment still houses servers, storage, networking, or compute infrastructure, Fort Worth may still classify it as a data center.

Modular Compute Classification

Fort Worth defines a data center functionally: a physical facility that houses IT infrastructure including servers, storage devices, and networking equipment used to store, process, and manage digital operations and data. That definition does not turn on campus size, water cooling, or a specific MW threshold.

Implication: a small modular compute installation can be a much lower-impact data center, but it may still be a data center. The use-determination package should describe the actual operating facts:

  • Maximum coincident load in kW/MW and phased ramp schedule.
  • Number and type of modules, racks, cabinets, and exterior equipment.
  • Cooling method, with explicit confirmation of no water cooling.
  • Generator, battery, UPS, transformer, and switchgear details.
  • Noise profile at the property line and nearest residential uses.
  • Water/sewer demand, including confirmation that no process cooling water or industrial wastewater discharge is expected.
  • Whether the compute use includes crypto mining; current Fort Worth proposals would prohibit crypto mining as a primary use.

Local Rules That Matter

Current zoning is favorable because K Heavy Industrial allows data centers by right under the existing use table. Current industrial standards still apply, including setbacks, height, landscaping, urban forestry, lighting, building, fire, stormwater, airport-height, utility, and permit review.

Pending Fort Worth rules are aimed at hyperscale-style impacts but would likely apply by use category. The major proposed standards include a 250-foot building setback from residential districts and uses, a 300-foot residential separation for standby generators, screening, acoustic barriers for rooftop cooling equipment, lighting controls near residential uses, 50-foot residential landscape buffers, closed-loop cooling, and data-center wastewater pretreatment controls.

The modular-compute opportunity is to be materially cleaner than the concern case:

Moratorium And Application Gate

Fort Worth has not yet adopted the 90-day data-center moratorium. On August 11, 2026, Council initiated the Texas Local Government Code Chapter 212 process. City staff stated that the August 11 vote did not impose the moratorium immediately; it started the notice, hearing, written-findings, and ordinance-reading process. The City's published timeline points to a first public hearing no later than November 10, 2026 and tentative final adoption on February 16, 2027. Final adoption would require a Council supermajority.

If adopted, the moratorium is described as a pause on accepting, processing, and approving new data-center applications or rezonings. City staff stated that the moratorium would not apply to vested projects, approved projects, or projects with zoning applications already submitted. For 4401 Deen, the zoning feasibility hinge is therefore not just "is K zoning permissive?" It is "what filing, if submitted before any moratorium is adopted, is sufficient to be treated as already submitted or vested?"

Separate from the future moratorium, Fort Worth adopted an immediate Chapter 14 application filing requirement on August 11. Staff described the requirement as applying to pre-development conferences, platting, replatting, building permits, and infrastructure plan review. It does not apply to annexation or pre-annexation requests. The rule requires a data-center development to provide proof of approval from the Public Utility Commission of Texas, ERCOT, or the relevant utility/grid-review process before the City processes the covered local applications.

Investor zoning read: the best near-term protective move is not a casual zoning question. It is a complete written use/classification and filing strategy that asks Fort Worth which bucket modular compute falls into and which filing preserves position before any moratorium. If the City treats the project as a data center, the Chapter 14 grid-approval gate may be the practical blocker even though K zoning allows the use.

Power And Utility Path

There is no Fort Worth MW/kW safe harbor in the data-center rules I found. Power thresholds matter mainly at the ERCOT/PUC/Oncor layer, not as a local zoning exemption.

Power band Utility/regulatory read Practical speed read
Below 1 MW Usually ordinary commercial service if local distribution capacity exists. Oncor says a project manager should contact new-service applicants within two business days after submittal; schedule depends on required poles, wire, transformer, easements, inspections, and meter setup.
1-10 MW Likely a larger commercial/distribution-capacity question. If onsite generation or DER is involved, Oncor's large DG/resource path becomes relevant. Oncor says large DG/grid-resource projects generally take 9-12 months, varying by project size. Impact studies may take 6-8 weeks and can identify upgrades with long-lead equipment.
10-25 MW Potentially custom feeder, transformer, switchgear, or substation work depending on nearby capacity. No published Oncor fixed timeline found. Expect prescreen, engineering study, cost estimate, easements/site work, equipment lead times, and possible outage-clearance windows.
25-74.9 MW ERCOT describes this as "medium-sized load" for certain load-forecast visibility. It is not the formal 75 MW large-load threshold under current PUCT large-load forecasting rule. Faster than 75 MW-plus only if Oncor has deliverable capacity nearby. Still politically sensitive because ERCOT tracks medium-sized loads in forecasting.
75 MW and above PUCT rule 16 TAC 25.370 defines a large load customer as expected non-coincident peak demand at a single site of at least 75 MW. ERCOT's Large Load Integration / Batch Zero framework applies at this scale. Slowest and most exposed. Expect ERCOT/TSP study, interconnection agreement, disclosures, financial security, direct interconnection costs, and possible transmission upgrades.

How they determine "data center power": ERCOT/PUC do not appear to classify a site as a data center based on zoning. They look at electrical load: contracted peak demand, expected non-coincident peak demand at the site, point of interconnection/service delivery, load ramp, modeling data, backup/curtailment capability, and whether the serving TDSP can attest to the load for ERCOT planning. Fort Worth, by contrast, looks at land use and facility function.

For 4401 Deen, a sub-25 MW, no-water-cooling, no-dedicated-substation concept is materially easier to explain than a hyperscale data center. It is not immune from local data-center regulation if the use is still compute infrastructure.

Noise And Water

Current Fort Worth industrial districts are described by the City as exempt from maximum sound levels. The pending data-center package would change that by requiring a 72-hour pre-development ambient noise study within 250 feet of residential districts or uses, limiting sound to no more than 5 dBA above ambient, requiring operator-funded mitigation, limiting routine generator testing to weekdays 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., and exempting emergency generator operation.

For modular compute, noise diligence should come before filing. The memo package should include module/HVAC sound data, transformer or switchgear sound if applicable, battery/UPS equipment details, generator specs if any, and a property-line acoustic model. Committing voluntarily to the emerging "ambient + 5 dBA" framework would directly answer the concern heard in Fort Worth meetings.

Water is the easier story if there is truly no water cooling. Fort Worth's proposed rules focus on closed-loop cooling and wastewater pretreatment because the public concern is evaporative cooling, chemical treatment, and discharge. A no-water-cooling project should provide a water memo showing domestic-only demand, no process cooling water, no cooling-tower chemicals, and no industrial wastewater discharge.

Local Planning Evidence

Looking back before the moratorium push, Fort Worth's record is not a straight anti-data-center pattern. Council repeatedly approved data-center entitlements when applicants narrowed the use, accepted site-plan and operating conditions, added buffers, and made an economic-benefit case. The pressure points that harden opposition are residential proximity, height and box form, generators and noise, water, dedicated substations, power-cap uncertainty, and perceived rush ahead of new rules. For 4401 Deen, the cleanest modular-compute strategy is to preserve the by-right K zoning advantage while voluntarily making the record look smaller, quieter, lower-load, and more constrained than the hyperscale cases.
